# Heuristics & Decision-Making — Curriculum Content Outlook

(Beginner-to-expert progression, compressed to 15 sections per the Researching-Outline method.)

**Section 1 — Foundations: Bounded Rationality and the Dual-Process Framework**

**Summary:** Introduces the field and its relevance to trend analysis: bounded rationality — Simon's thesis that humans satisfice under cognitive limits rather than optimize, establishing heuristics as the adaptive response to an information-rich world; the dual-process framework — fast, automatic, associative System 1 versus slow, deliberate System 2, presented with its modern scientific caveats (a useful metaphor rather than a literal two-brain architecture); heuristics defined — effort-reducing judgment strategies that are usually adaptive but predictably biased in specific environments; the two scholarly traditions contrasted — the heuristics-and-biases program (Tversky-Kahneman: heuristics as error sources) versus ecological rationality (Gigerenzer: heuristics as fitted tools whose performance depends on environment structure), with the debate's practical lesson that context determines whether a heuristic helps; why trend analysts need this course — demand spikes and adoption waves are mass expressions of judgment heuristics, and the analyst's own forecasts are heuristic-laden too; the analysis targets named — social proof, herd behavior, FOMO, scarcity, anchoring, and availability as the bias family behind most trend surges; evidence culture — experimental methods of judgment research, effect sizes, and the replication-crisis reassessment as standing context for every finding used later; the decision-ecology frame — environments (platforms, markets) that exploit or protect against biases; and the course map from specific heuristics through social mechanisms, market-level cascades, and debiasing to the ethics of influence and a capstone demand-spike analysis.

**Absorbed Skill:** Explain bounded rationality and satisficing as the origin of heuristic judgment, apply the dual-process framework with its scientific caveats, contrast heuristics-and-biases with ecological-rationality accounts and predict when heuristics help versus hurt, identify the bias family behind observed demand surges, and assess judgment findings with effect-size and replication literacy.

**Section 2 — The Core Heuristics: Availability, Representativeness, Anchoring, and Affect**

**Summary:** Teaches the canonical heuristics in working depth: availability — judging frequency and probability by ease of recall, its drivers (recency, vividness, media coverage, emotional intensity), and its trend consequences (recent viral events inflating perceived prevalence, media-shaped risk and demand perception); representativeness — judging by similarity to prototypes, producing base-rate neglect, conjunction fallacies (the Linda problem), and insensitivity to sample size, with trend applications (judging a product by fit to a trend prototype rather than market base rates); anchoring and adjustment — arbitrary anchors shifting estimates, anchored pricing and reference-price effects in commerce, and first-mover anchoring in trend narratives; the affect heuristic — feelings as information, how emotional tags on mental representations drive risk and preference judgments, and mood-congruent trend perception; simulation heuristic and counterfactual thinking at overview; recognition heuristic (Goldstein-Gigerenzer) as the ecological-rationality showcase; heuristic selection — which heuristic fires when, and the substitution principle (answering an easier question than the one asked); the biases each heuristic produces stated as testable predictions; cross-heuristic interaction in real judgment (an available, representative, anchored, affect-laden trend call); and practice — decomposing a real consumer judgment or analyst forecast into its heuristic components with debiasing implications.

**Absorbed Skill:** Predict availability-driven prevalence misjudgments from recency and media exposure, diagnose representativeness errors including base-rate neglect and small-sample insensitivity, identify anchoring effects in pricing and narrative formation, apply the affect heuristic to emotion-driven preference judgments, and decompose real consumer and analyst judgments into component heuristics with testable bias predictions.

**Section 3 — The Bias Landscape: Taxonomy, Evidence, and Replication Realities**

**Summary:** Maps the wider bias family with scientific discipline: a working taxonomy — information-processing biases (confirmation, survivorship, selection), judgment biases (overconfidence, planning fallacy, optimism, hindsight), social biases (covered in later sections), and decision biases (loss aversion, framing, status quo, sunk cost); confirmation bias in depth — biased search, interpretation, and memory, its role in trend narrative lock-in, and why it resists simple instruction-based debiasing; loss aversion and prospect theory — reference dependence, diminishing sensitivity, the kinked value function, probability weighting, and the endowment effect, with the documented effect-size debates; framing effects — attribute and goal frames, gain/loss frames in marketing language; overconfidence family — calibration findings, the better-than-average effect, and expert-forecast calibration (Tetlock's findings); sunk-cost and status-quo biases in adoption and churn decisions; the replication crisis confronted — which landmark findings weakened under replication (ego depletion, social-priming effects), what survived (many heuristics-and-biases staples), and the general lesson of consulting meta-analyses over single studies; effect-size discipline — small-to-moderate effects compounding across populations versus individual determinism; bias-blind-spot and naïve realism — the finding that people see bias in others more than themselves, aimed squarely at the analyst; and using the taxonomy — a bias-audit checklist for interpreting observed consumer behavior spikes.

**Absorbed Skill:** Classify observed judgment patterns using an information/judgment/social/decision bias taxonomy, apply prospect theory constructs (reference dependence, loss aversion, probability weighting) to consumer choices, audit trend narratives for confirmation, survivorship, and hindsight biases, evaluate behavioral findings via meta-analyses and replication status rather than single studies, and run bias audits that include the analyst's own blind spot.

**Section 4 — Social Proof and the Principles of Influence**

**Summary:** Covers the most trend-relevant social mechanism: social proof defined — using others' behavior as evidence about reality, strongest under uncertainty and similarity; Cialdini's influence principles in working depth — reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity, and unity, each with its experimental base and marketing instantiations; the classic evidence — Asch's conformity experiments, Sherif's norm-formation studies, informational versus normative social influence distinguished; social proof in digital environments — review counts and ratings, bestseller and trending badges, follower counts, purchase notifications, waitlists, and 'X people are viewing this' mechanics, with platform-native amplification of proof signals; manufactured social proof — fake reviews, bot engagement, purchased followers, astroturfed trends, and detection heuristics (review-timing bursts, language patterns, rating distributions); similarity and the reference-group effect — proof from in-groups persuades more (creator communities, local trends); the numbers-versus-credibility trade-off — when large counts backfire (over-commercialization signals); authority and expertise cues — verification badges, expert endorsements, credential signaling; liking and parasocial influence — creator affinity as a persuasion channel (connecting to the attention course); ethical deployment — honest proof versus fabrication, with regulatory exposure (FTC review rules and equivalents); and analysis practice — auditing a product's or trend's social-proof architecture and estimating its contribution to observed demand.

**Absorbed Skill:** Analyze social-proof mechanisms across digital touchpoints (ratings, badges, counts, notifications), distinguish informational from normative influence in observed conformity, detect manufactured social proof via timing, language, and distribution signals, apply Cialdini's principles to real marketing architectures with ethical guardrails, and audit a trend's social-proof stack estimating its demand contribution.

**Section 5 — Herd Behavior and Information Cascades**

**Summary:** Teaches market-level herding: information cascades formalized — the Bikhchandani-Hirshleifer-Welch result that rational agents ignoring private signals to follow predecessors can lock in wrong choices, with the fragility of cascades (a little contrary information can break them); Banerjee's model of rational herding under payoff uncertainty; cascade conditions — sequential decisions, observable actions but hidden information, and why platforms maximize cascade potential (public counts, visible adoption, feed ordering); herding versus social proof distinguished — cascades as the dynamic process, proof as the static cue; empirical cascade domains — financial bubbles and crashes, bank runs, fashion cycles, technology adoption, panic buying (documented episodes), and queue phenomena; contrarian value — information embedded in prices versus cascade distortion; anti-cascades and backlash dynamics — when visible over-adoption triggers avoidance (saturation, cringe, counter-signaling), connecting to trend lifecycles; cascade prediction research — early-behavior features of cascading versus stalling content, with modest predictive power per the literature; network position effects — cascades need bridge nodes and clusters (connecting to the network course); intervention points — slowing harmful cascades (circuit breakers, friction, disclosure) and their evidence; and the trend analyst's application — classifying an observed demand wave as information-driven growth versus cascade-driven herding versus manufactured astroturf, using observable signatures.

**Absorbed Skill:** Apply information-cascade models to explain rational herding and its fragility, identify cascade-enabling platform conditions (sequential observable decisions), distinguish information-driven growth from cascade-driven herding in demand waves, analyze anti-cascade backlash dynamics at saturation, and evaluate cascade intervention points (friction, disclosure, circuit breakers) with evidence awareness.

**Section 6 — FOMO, Scarcity, and Urgency**

**Summary:** Covers the acceleration mechanisms behind purchase spikes: FOMO defined and measured — fear of missing out as trait and state, its association with social-media use and well-being findings, and its role as demand accelerator when opportunity seems ephemeral; scarcity mechanics — commodity theory roots, limited-quantity and limited-time scarcity, the psychological pathways (perceived value increase, competitive arousal, reactance when freedom to choose is threatened); experimental evidence on scarcity claims — what replicates (perceived-value and urgency effects) versus contested findings, with effect-size honesty; drop culture and release mechanics — sneaker/fashion drops, limited editions, waitlist mechanics, and appointment scarcity as engineered FOMO at industrial scale; urgency interfaces — countdown timers, low-stock messages, 'selling fast' badges, and their dark-pattern classification when false; reactance theory — psychological reactance as the backfire mechanism when scarcity pressure is perceived as manipulation; FOMO in trend adoption — joining trends late for belonging, the visible-participation evidence (hashtag surges, challenge joins), and FOMO's short half-life versus durable adoption; measurement practice — detecting FOMO-driven demand signatures (spike shape, social-media mention correlation, resale premiums, rapid post-peak collapse); regulatory lines — false-scarcity enforcement (consumer-protection cases against fake countdowns and stock claims); ethical deployment — real scarcity honestly communicated versus manufactured pressure; and the analysis frame — scarcity and FOMO as the accelerants layered on social-proof and cascade dynamics in a demand spike.

**Absorbed Skill:** Distinguish trait and state FOMO and their demand-acceleration roles, analyze scarcity mechanics (quantity, time, drop culture) with reactance-backfire awareness, detect false-urgency dark patterns against regulatory lines, identify FOMO-driven demand signatures in spike shape and resale data, and position scarcity as an accelerant within multi-mechanism demand spikes.

**Section 7 — Choice Architecture, Nudges, and Defaults**

**Summary:** Teaches the environment-design layer: choice architecture defined — every presentation of options embeds a design that shapes choice, with no neutral option; the Nudge framework — libertarian paternalism, the Thaler-Sunstein toolkit (defaults, framing, social-norm feedback, salience, friction reduction), and MINDSPACE/EAST as applied taxonomies; defaults in depth — the powerful documented effects (organ donation, retirement enrollment, privacy settings, green energy), why defaults stick (status-quo bias, endorsement inference, effort), and default design for trends (opt-in versus opt-out participation mechanics); friction and sludge — adding or removing steps as behavioral levers, with one-click purchasing and checkout-streamlining evidence; salience and ordering — position effects, decoy and compromise effects (the asymmetrical-dominance finding), and assortment architecture; social-norm feedback — descriptive versus injunctive norms, the boomerang effect when norms are miscommunicated, and 'most people like you' messaging; personalization as choice architecture — recommendation systems narrowing the option set (connecting to the recommendation-architecture course); nudge evidence quality — meta-analytic effect sizes, heterogeneity across contexts, and the replication-literate reading; sludge audits and choice-architecture audits as analytic methods; the nudge-versus-boost debate — designing environments versus building competencies (Gigerenzer's position); and applying architecture analysis — auditing a purchase funnel or platform for the choice-architecture levers driving its conversion and adoption patterns.

**Absorbed Skill:** Audit choice environments for defaults, friction, salience, decoys, and norm-feedback levers, predict default and status-quo effects on adoption and conversion, design and evaluate nudges with meta-analytic effect-size and boomerang-effect awareness, distinguish nudge from boost strategies, and analyze purchase funnels and platform architectures as choice-architecture systems.

**Section 8 — Emotion, Motivation, and the Hot-Cold Empathy Gap**

**Summary:** Covers the affective drivers: emotion in decision-making — the move from bias-as-error to emotion-as-input (somatic-marker lineage at overview, affect-as-information in depth), with integral versus incidental emotion distinguished; incidental-emotion effects — anxiety increasing information-seeking and safety-product demand, anger increasing risk-taking and punitive choices, sadness increasing disposition-to-change and valuation shifts (the documented 'helpless' pricing effects), excitement driving impulsive purchase; emotional arousal and narrow focus — high-arousal states degrading deliberation, the hot-cold empathy gap (underestimating future hot-state behavior), and implications for impulse-spike analysis; emotional contagion — mood spreading through networks and feeds, its measurement, and its amplification of trend emotions; mood and trend perception — analysts' own affect biasing trend calls; motivation systems — regulatory focus (promotion versus prevention orientation shaping which trends resonate), needs-based adoption (belonging, status, identity as the deep drivers under observed purchases), and self-determination at overview; identity and expressive consumption — purchases as identity signals, the symbolic-value framework, and identity-consistent trend adoption; anticipatory emotions — expected regret and nostalgia as purchase drivers; measurement practice — emotion signals in social text (connecting to the sentiment course) as demand-spike correlates, with emotion-label validity caveats; and the analysis synthesis — mapping a demand spike's emotional signature (fear, excitement, belonging) to its mechanism family.

**Absorbed Skill:** Distinguish integral from incidental emotion effects and predict their choice consequences, identify arousal-driven impulse patterns and hot-cold empathy gaps in consumer behavior, analyze emotional contagion in feeds and networks, apply regulatory-focus and identity-signaling frameworks to trend resonance, and map demand-spike emotional signatures to mechanism families using sentiment signals with validity caveats.

**Section 9 — Biases in Trend Adoption and Diffusion Psychology**

**Summary:** Connects the bias toolkit to trend dynamics specifically: the bandwagon effect — adoption accelerating with adoption share, distinguished from genuine network externalities and from information cascades, with measurement approaches; novelty-seeking versus conformity — the two-sided adoption psychology (early adopters' distinctiveness needs versus majority safety-seeking) bridging to diffusion-of-innovations categories (detailed in course 12); the hype cycle — technology-triggered expectation inflation, peak of inflated expectations, trough of disillusionment, and slope of enlightenment as an institutional-level bias pattern, with its heuristic status acknowledged; planning fallacy and optimism in trend timing — why organizations systematically misjudge adoption speed in both directions (too early or too late); survivorship and selection biases in trend evidence — visible successes hiding the base rate of failed trends, and the availability-driven overestimation of trend frequency; narrative bias — stories as cognitive frames that make trends feel inevitable (hindsight bias in trend reports); recency and extrapolation — linear-projecting recent growth (the documented tendency to expect continuation), and mean-reversion ignorance; status-quo and adoption inertia — the flip side explaining slow starts; network externalities and coordination — adoption as coordination game with tipping points; the bias-interaction model of a trend wave — novelty-seekers start it, social proof and cascades scale it, FOMO and scarcity accelerate it, and saturation triggers reactance and anti-cascade; and the trend-analyst's bias audit — correcting one's own wave interpretation for availability, narrative, and survivorship distortions.

**Absorbed Skill:** Distinguish bandwagon effects from network externalities and information cascades in adoption data, apply novelty-conformity dynamics to adoption-wave psychology, use the hype cycle as a scoped institutional-bias heuristic, correct trend interpretation for survivorship, narrative, hindsight, and extrapolation biases, and model trend waves as interactions of novelty-seeking, social proof, FOMO, and reactance.

**Section 10 — Group Decision-Making and Collective Behavior**

**Summary:** Scales from individual to collective judgment: group polarization — deliberation shifting groups toward more extreme positions, its mechanisms (persuasive arguments, social comparison) and online amplification evidence; groupthink — Janis' syndrome (cohesion, insulation, self-censorship, illusion of unanimity) with its classic cases and modern critiques, applied to trend-committee decisions and brand teams; wisdom-of-crowds — the Galton lineage, aggregation conditions (independence, diversity, decentralization), and when crowds fail (correlated errors, cascades breaking independence); prediction markets and collective forecasting — documented calibration performance versus expert panels; collective effervescence — Durkheim's concept for shared emotional arousal at events (launches, drops, livestreams) as a purchase-spike mechanism; crowd behavior modernized — from Le Bon's contagion metaphor to emergent-norm and social-identity approaches, with panic-buying as coordinated (not irrational) behavior under scarcity beliefs; committee forecasting failures — why group trend calls degrade (dominance, anchoring on first speakers, shared information bias) and structured alternatives (Delphi, nominal groups, prediction aggregation); organizational herding — firms copying firms (mimetic isomorphism, the DiMaggio-Powell finding) as trend amplification at market level; social-movement participation — threshold models of joining (Granovetter 1978) connecting individual heuristics to collective action; measuring collective judgment — aggregating social signals without cascade contamination; and the analyst's group practice — designing trend-review processes immune to the failure modes covered.

**Absorbed Skill:** Diagnose group polarization and groupthink dynamics in teams and online communities, apply wisdom-of-crowds aggregation conditions to social-signal measurement, analyze collective effervescence and panic-buying as coordinated crowd behavior, design structured group forecasting (Delphi, prediction aggregation) immune to anchoring and shared-information bias, and explain organizational herding as market-level trend amplification.

**Section 11 — Judging Evidence: Experiments, Effect Sizes, and the Replication Landscape**

**Summary:** Instills the evidence skills the rest of the course assumes: experimental methods in judgment research — lab versus field experiments, vignette designs and their external-validity limits, incentive-compatible designs; effect sizes and practical significance — why statistically significant small effects still matter at population scale yet rarely determine individual behavior; meta-analysis literacy — reading forest plots, heterogeneity, publication bias (funnel asymmetry), and multilab replication projects as the current best evidence; the replication crisis mapped — categories of findings (large-priming-era effects that failed, robust heuristics-and-biases staples, contested loss-aversion magnitudes) with the Open Science reforms (preregistration, registered reports) as context; p-hacking and HARKing recognition — spotting methodological red flags in cited studies; external validity for applied use — lab bias effects translating to market behavior, the field-experiment literature, and industry A/B evidence as the applied gold standard; observational-data pitfalls — inferring bias mechanisms from sales data requires ruling out confounds (marketing spend, seasonality, supply); pre-analysis thinking — specifying what evidence would confirm versus falsify a bias explanation for an observed spike; Bayesian updating of beliefs about mechanisms — prior strength from theory and evidence quality; the consultancy-claim audit — evaluating vendor and thought-leadership claims about behavioral effects; and building a personal evidence standard — a checklist for admitting any bias mechanism into an analysis.

**Absorbed Skill:** Evaluate judgment research by design, effect size, and external validity, read meta-analyses and multilab replications for current best evidence, spot p-hacking, HARKing, and publication-bias red flags, separate observational confounds from genuine bias mechanisms in market data, and apply a personal evidence checklist before admitting bias explanations into trend analysis.

**Section 12 — Debiasing and Decision Hygiene**

**Summary:** Teaches improving judgment, including the analyst's own: the debiasing evidence — what works (structured techniques, external viewpoint, incentives for accuracy in some contexts) versus what mostly fails (one-shot bias warnings, general education about biases), per the Morewedge-lineage review; cognitive-forcing strategies — consider-the-opposite, devil's advocacy, premortems, and checklist culture (Gawande lineage) with their documented effects and implementation decay; reference-class forecasting — the outside-view method (Flyvbjerg's work), base-rate discipline for trend-timing estimates; decision hygiene protocols — independent estimates before discussion (killing anchoring), blind analysis, and structured aggregation; forecasting tournaments and the superforecaster findings — practices of accurate forecasters (granular probability thinking, active open-mindedness, frequent updating) as trainable decision hygiene; probability calibration training — documented improvement from brief training; choice-architecture self-application — designing one's own decision environments (defaults for analysis workflows, friction against impulsive conclusions); organizational debiasing — red teams, pre-mortem rituals, and prediction markets inside firms, with the political realities; the analyst's specific risks — trend-call overconfidence, narrative lock-in, client-pleasing bias, and recency in report writing; measurement of debiasing effectiveness — tracking forecast calibration and decision-outcome quality over time; humility engineering — confidence language standards in trend reports (calibrated verbal probabilities); and building a personal and team decision-hygiene system with the course's full bias inventory.

**Absorbed Skill:** Apply cognitive-forcing strategies (consider-the-opposite, premortem, checklists) with evidence-based expectations, run reference-class and outside-view forecasting for trend-timing estimates, implement decision-hygiene protocols (independent estimates, blind analysis, structured aggregation), adopt superforecaster practices including calibrated probability language, and build team debiasing systems with tracked calibration outcomes.

**Section 13 — Biases in Data and Measurement: What Judgment Science Does to Your Trend Data**

**Summary:** Turns the toolkit on the analyst's inputs: social-media data biases — self-selection into posting, platform-visibility bias (algorithmic amplification shapes what data exists), bot and astroturf contamination, and the vocal-minority problem (skewed participation distributions); survey biases in trend measurement — social-desirability responding (stated versus revealed preference gaps), acquiescence, question-order and framing effects in survey instruments, and non-response bias; recall bias — consumers misremembering adoption timing and motivations, corrupting survey-based trend histories; search-data biases — availability-driven search, news-cycle contamination of search indices, and the interpretation gap between search and purchase; sales-data confounds — promotion calendars, supply constraints, and competitor actions mimicking bias-driven spikes; observational identification problems — distinguishing social proof from genuine quality signals, cascades from coordinated campaigns, and FOMO spikes from supply-limited rationing; measurement-instrument bias — sentiment models inheriting training-data biases, engagement metrics rewarding outrage (connecting to the attention course); the analyst's own contribution — confirmation-driven query construction, selective time-windowing, and narrative-first analysis; triangulation doctrine — converging evidence from independent data sources with different bias profiles; bias-mapping documentation — recording each data source's bias profile alongside its findings; and simulation practice — testing whether a bias mechanism can quantitatively explain an observed data pattern before asserting it.

**Absorbed Skill:** Audit social, survey, search, and sales data for their characteristic bias profiles, separate bias-driven patterns from confounds (promotions, supply, coordination) in demand data, identify measurement-instrument biases in sentiment and engagement metrics, apply cross-source triangulation with documented bias profiles, and quantitatively test whether a bias mechanism can explain an observed pattern before asserting it.

**Section 14 — Ethics, Dark Patterns, and Regulation of Behavioral Influence**

**Summary:** Covers responsible application: the influence spectrum — from honest persuasion (informing choice) through nudging (shaping default environments) to manipulation (exploiting biases against users' interests), with the autonomy and welfare criteria distinguishing them; dark patterns taxonomy — Mathur-lineage and Gray-lineage classifications (confirmshaming, false urgency, hidden costs, roach motels, forced continuity, social-proof fabrication), their measured prevalence in e-commerce and apps, and their documented consumer harms; legal and regulatory landscape — FTC Act unfair/deceptive standards applied to fake reviews and false scarcity, the EU Digital Services Act and consumer-protection directives addressing dark patterns, state-level US laws, and enforcement cases as precedent; platform governance — app-store and platform policies against manipulative design; vulnerable populations — children's susceptibility to persuasive design (age-appropriate design codes), addiction-adjacent mechanics, and financial-harm contexts; practitioner ethics codes — behavioral-science applications' professional standards and disclosure norms; the asymmetry problem — defenders using the same knowledge for friction, warnings, and cooling-off periods (scam interruption, purchase-delay patterns); ethical influence design — honest scarcity, transparent defaults, opt-in amplification, and the business case (trust as durable asset, dark-pattern churn evidence); whistleblowing and professional responsibility — when to refuse a manipulative brief; trend-analysis ethics — not manufacturing demand spikes through coordinated manipulation while claiming to merely observe them; and the ethics checklist — a decision tool for evaluating any behavioral-influence deployment across honesty, autonomy, vulnerability, and reversibility dimensions.

**Absorbed Skill:** Classify influence tactics along the persuasion-nudge-manipulation spectrum with autonomy criteria, identify dark patterns by taxonomy and assess prevalence evidence, apply the regulatory landscape (FTC, DSA, state laws) to influence deployments, design defensive friction and honest-influence alternatives, and operate an ethics checklist covering honesty, autonomy, vulnerability, and reversibility before any behavioral campaign.

**Section 15 — Synthesis and Capstone: Behavioral Analysis of Demand Spikes and Trend Adoption**

**Summary:** Prepares expert integration: the consolidated behavioral model of trend adoption — individual heuristics (availability, affect, representativeness) filtered through social mechanisms (social proof, cascades, group dynamics), accelerated by scarcity-FOMO-urgency, shaped by choice architecture, and expressed in emotional and identity-consistent purchases; the demand-spike analysis protocol — the full workflow: characterize the spike (shape, timing, magnitude), map candidate mechanisms (proof, cascade, FOMO, marketing confound, supply artifact), gather mechanism-specific evidence (social sentiment, proof-signal changes, adoption sequencing, resale premiums), rule out confounds, quantify mechanism plausibility, and forecast persistence (FOMO spikes collapse; genuine-value adoption persists); mechanism-signature library — the observable fingerprints of each mechanism (cascade: sequential adoption following visible leaders; scarcity-FOMO: countdown correlation and resale premium; social proof: rating/badge changes preceding uptake; emotional: sentiment-spike coincidence); cross-course integration — network analysis (course 2) for cascade paths, time-series methods (course 3) for spike characterization and persistence forecasting, NLP (course 1) for emotion and narrative signals, and diffusion theory (course 12) for adoption-stage placement; intervention design — applying choice architecture and ethical influence to amplify genuine trends or damp harmful cascades; decision hygiene throughout — the analyst's own biases audited at every stage with calibration-tracked forecasts; case synthesis — full worked analyses of real spike types (product drops, panic buying, viral adoption, manufactured astroturf); reporting standards — mechanism claims with evidence grades and uncertainty; and the capstone — a complete behavioral trend analysis: take a real or simulated demand spike, execute the full protocol from characterization through mechanism identification and confound ruling to persistence forecasting and ethical intervention design, delivering a decision-hygiene-audited report.

**Absorbed Skill:** Execute a complete demand-spike analysis protocol from characterization through mechanism identification to persistence forecasting, match observed data signatures to mechanism fingerprints (cascade, social proof, FOMO, confounds), integrate network, time-series, and NLP methods into behavioral analysis, design ethical choice-architecture interventions, and deliver mechanism-graded, bias-audited, calibrated trend-adoption reports.

## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Covers the generations the course serves: generational framing with caveats — cohort definitions (Gen Z roughly late-1990s to early-2010s births, Gen Alpha after), the methodological limits of generational categories (within-cohort diversity, marketing-invented boundaries), and using generations as rough analytic lenses rather than essences; networked socialization — boyd's findings on teens' networked publics (persistent, searchable, spreadable, invisible audiences) and the collapsed-context social life they produce; the 'hanging out, messing around, geeking out' model (Ito lineage) — informal learning pathways online; platform biography — Gen Z's formative platforms (YouTube, Snapchat, TikTok, Discord, gaming) and Gen Alpha's (YouTube Kids lineage, Roblox, TikTok saturation, AI companions) shaping communication norms; documented cohort patterns with evidence grading — mental-health and social-media debates (the contested causality, Haidt-lineage claims versus mixed evidence), climate anxiety, economic pessimism shaping consumption, identity fluidity and expression, and humor styles (absurdist, ironic, post-ironic layers); digital-native competencies — platform fluency, multi-account identity management (finstas, alts), and media-savviness including bullshit detection; consumption attitudes — authenticity demands, anti-advertising literacy, resale and thrift cultures, creator-over-brand trust; Gen Alpha specifics — the first fully AI-mediated cohort, gaming-world socialization (Roblox as playground), and parental-mediation differences; research ethics with minors — heightened consent, assent, and protection requirements; method implications — meeting these cohorts in their spaces (games, ephemeral media) with their communication forms (memes, video, slang); and the reflexivity requirement — the analyst's generational position shaping what looks normal.

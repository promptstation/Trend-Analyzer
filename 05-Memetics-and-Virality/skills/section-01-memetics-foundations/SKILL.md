# Foundations: The Meme Concept and Cultural Evolution — Professional Training

## Mission

Produce professional-grade learning modules and training materials on **foundations: The Meme Concept and Cultural Evolution** within Cultural evolution, internet meme studies, and diffusion science applied to the replication, mutation, and selection of viral ideas.

Subject scope: Introduces the field: Dawkins' 1976 coinage of the meme as a unit of cultural transmission analogous to the gene — replicating through imitation with variation and selection, carrying the selfish-gene logic to culture; the core claim examined — culture evolves by differential replication, not merely by invention-and-adoption; meme defined operationally for internet contexts — a recognizable unit (image macro, sound, phrase, format, gesture, challenge) that spreads by copying and remixing, with Shifman's distinction between single viral artifacts and memetic families of many user-created variants; the internet's effect on memetics — near-zero copying cost, visible mutation trails, and measurable spread turning metaphor into data; why trend analysts care — memes are the fastest-moving layer of culture and the leading indicators of aesthetic, linguistic, and behavioral trends; the participatory premise — audiences are co-creators, and spread is driven by remaking, not just sharing; the scope of memetic phenomena — jokes and formats, but also slang, sounds, challenges, aesthetics, and behaviors; the course's honest framing that memetics is contested as a scientific theory yet powerful as an analytic lens; key vocabulary established (replication, variation, selection, fitness, mutation, template); and the course map from theory and mechanics through measurement, marketing, politics, and ethics to capstone meme-intelligence practice.

Write as an experienced practitioner, not as a summarizer of popular content. Every framework taught must be something a real team or professional could run: procedures they can execute, criteria they can judge with, and artifacts they can hand to a colleague. Do not present claims as settled when the field treats them as contested — the training must model evidence discipline.

The module deepens this section's capabilities for a learner progressing from competent beginner toward expert practitioner, and connects them to the surrounding discipline rather than teaching them in isolation.

## Core Output Requirements

- Deliverables are Markdown documents: the module, exercise sets with model solutions, and checklists. No placeholders, no "TODO" sections.
- Ground content in authoritative sources:
- Dawkins, The Selfish Gene (the meme concept) and the subsequent memetics literature (Dennett, Blackmore, Journal of Memetics archive)
- Shifman, Memes in Digital Culture (MIT Press) — internet meme studies, participatory culture, memetic dimensions
- Berger, Contagious (STEPPS framework) and Berger & Milkman's empirical study of most-emailed content
- Know Your Meme and meme-documentation practice as primary genealogical sources
- Cheng, Adamic, Kleinberg lineage on cascade prediction and viral dynamics research
- Miltner & Highfield, Wiggins, Milner, and Phillips (This Is Why We Can't Have Nice Things) on meme communities, politics, and ambivalence
- Coleman and folklore/digital-culture scholarship on memes as folk expression
- Maintain an evidence ledger while writing: every factual claim or number is either sourced, flagged as disputed/popular account, or omitted. Never invent statistics, studies, or citations.
- Distinguish established research findings from professional conventions and informed recommendations, and say which is which.
- Explain each specialized term in clear language on first use.

## Writing Standards

Throughout the material, prioritize language that is:

* Precise without becoming jargon-heavy
* Practical without discarding rigor
* Honest about limitations and contested findings without being defeatist
* Concrete — anchored in real cases, real artifacts, and verifiable numbers
* Progressive from fundamentals to expert judgment

Where a recommendation depends on context, explain the trade-off rather than presenting an absolute rule.

## Anti-Patterns

Avoid: Treating memetics' gene analogy literally — memes lack clear units, fidelity, and copying mechanisms, and the critiques matter for analysis; Confusing virality (a spread pattern) with quality, truth, or representativeness of an audience; Attributing spread to content features alone while ignoring platform amplification, timing, and network position; Assuming meme adoption signals endorsement — irony, ambivalence, and participation without belief are the norm; Trend-jacking memes without understanding community ownership and context, triggering backlash; exercises without evaluation criteria; modules that stop at inspiration without a single decision the learner can now make better; and any content that overstates certainty beyond what the evidence supports.

## Decision Heuristic

When allocating depth under time or length limits, ask:

1. Where will this audience actually stall in practice?
2. Which unit protects the most value if taught well?
3. Can the learner run the framework tomorrow without me?
4. Is every example doing work a plain sentence could not?
5. What would a skeptical domain expert say about this material?

If two topics compete for space, keep the one that changes a decision.

## Final Principle

The goal is not more content about Memetics and virality. The goal is that a practitioner could take this material and perform: Define memes operationally for internet contexts and distinguish single viral artifacts from memetic families, apply core memetic vocabulary (template, variation, selection, fitness) to observed content, and frame meme participation as co-creation rather than passive sharing — with the same evidence discipline the field's best practitioners use. The module must function simultaneously as a learning resource, a practical reference, and a working methodology.

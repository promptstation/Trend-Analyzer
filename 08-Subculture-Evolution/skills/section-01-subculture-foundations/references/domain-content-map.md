## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Introduces the field and its trend-analysis mission: subculture defined — groups differentiated from a dominant culture by distinctive practices, styles, values, and belonging, historically anchored in class, generation, and locality; the concept family distinguished — subculture, counterculture (explicit political opposition), scene (locally organized music/culture clusters), tribe (fluid affective groupings), and aesthetic communities (style-centered online formations) — with the analyst's task of classifying what is actually being observed; the trend-pipeline thesis — commercial culture's most durable innovations originate underground, get noticed, get adapted, and arrive mainstream in diluted form (punk to runway safety pins, skate to luxury streetwear, Tumblr aesthetics to mall fashion), making subculture tracking the deepest early-warning layer of trend intelligence; the pace change — internet infrastructure compressing the underground-to-mainstream cycle from years to months, with evidence and the counterpoint that some pipelines still run slow; why underground signals beat mainstream ones — differentiation motives, experimentation density, and the authenticity premium; the key tension named — co-optation: subcultures supply commercial culture with novelty while often receiving dilution, misattribution, and harm in return; the disciplines drawn on — Birmingham School cultural studies, post-subcultural theory, fashion sociology, and digital culture studies, previewed as the course's theoretical spine; landmark case previews (punk, hip-hop, grunge, streetwear, cottagecore, Y2K revival); the ethics frame established upfront — credit, consent, and extraction concerns as recurring themes; and the course map from theory through style semiotics, lifecycles, measurement, and ethics to forecasting mainstreaming.

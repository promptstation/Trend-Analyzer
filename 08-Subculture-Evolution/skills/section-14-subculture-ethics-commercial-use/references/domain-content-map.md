## Absorbed capabilities this module must produce
1. Distinguish appropriation, exchange, and appreciation using power-asymmetry and credit criteria.
2. Assess extraction harms across economic, cultural, social, and psychological dimensions.
3. Design credit and compensation practices (collaboration, licensing, attribution) for commercial use, anticipate backlash triggers and the visibility paradox in trend reporting.
4. Operate an ethics decision framework covering origin, harm, consent, and visibility before commercial deployment.

## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Confronts the field's central moral problem: cultural appropriation defined and distinguished — the power-asymmetry framework (dominant-culture adoption of marginalized-culture elements without credit, context, or compensation) versus exchange and appreciation, with the debates over boundaries; the extraction economy — how commercial actors historically harvested subcultural novelty while originators received dilution and misattribution (Dapper Dan's reversed-appropriation case, hip-hop fashion's credit failures, AAVE-to-marketing-pipeline controversies); harm taxonomy — economic (uncompensated value transfer), cultural (meaning stripping, sacred/profane misuse), social (stereotype reinforcement, backlash against origin community), and psychological (identity commodification); credit and attribution practice — origin documentation, creator compensation models (consulting fees, collaborations, revenue shares), and the licensing-versus-tribute spectrum; community consent and relationship — engaging scenes as partners versus mining them as data, the broker ethics variance, and long-term relationship value; backlash dynamics — documented call-out campaigns (Twitter/TikTok accountability waves), their triggers (uncredited adoption, sacred-symbol misuse, stereotype amplification), and their commercial costs; the trend-report ethics — writing about subcultures for commercial audiences without exposing vulnerable scenes to tourism or exploitation (the visibility paradox: coverage accelerates both commercial opportunity and dilution); legal dimensions at awareness — IP limits on style, trademark issues in scene names, right-of-publicity, and why legal compliance is far below ethical sufficiency; positionality and standing — who may ethically commercialize which signals, and the honest self-assessment for analysts and brands; redemptive models — documented collaborations that compensated and credited originators with mutual benefit; and the ethics decision framework — origin tracing, harm assessment, consent and compensation options, visibility consequences, and documentation before any commercial use of a subcultural signal.

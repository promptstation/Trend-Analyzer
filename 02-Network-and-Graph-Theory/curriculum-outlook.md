# Network & Graph Theory — Curriculum Content Outlook

(Beginner-to-expert progression, compressed to 15 sections per the Researching-Outline method.)

**Section 1 — Foundations: Networks as the Structure of Spread**

**Summary:** Introduces the field and its claim on trend analysis: graphs and networks defined — nodes, edges, and the type system (directed, weighted, signed, bipartite, multilayer) — with the course's orienting thesis that ideas, content, and behaviors spread through relational structure rather than through undifferentiated populations; network thinking versus attribute thinking — the relational paradigm where position and connection carry explanatory power; exemplar networks across domains (follow graphs, repost cascades, co-mention networks, citation graphs, infrastructure); where network data comes from — platform graphs and their access realities, surveys, interaction logs, and co-occurrence construction — with each source's sampling caveats; representation fundamentals — adjacency matrices, adjacency lists, edge lists — and basic descriptives (size, density, degree distributions at first look); analysis, visualization, and modeling distinguished as three modes of network work; the tooling landscape (NetworkX, igraph, graph-tool, Gephi) at orientation level; and the course map from static structure through position and community to dynamics, diffusion, and trend intelligence.

**Absorbed Skill:** Represent relational data as properly typed graphs with matrix and list representations, distinguish network explanations from attribute-based explanations, construct networks from platform and co-occurrence data with sampling awareness, compute and interpret basic graph descriptives, and select analysis tools matched to task and scale.

**Section 2 — Graph Theory Fundamentals**

**Summary:** Builds the structural toolkit: walks, trails, paths, and cycles; connectivity and components — weak versus strong connectivity in directed graphs, and giant components; distance, eccentricity, diameter, and average path length with their measurement subtleties in disconnected graphs; trees, spanning trees, and minimum spanning trees as extraction tools; bipartite graphs and one-mode projection — the standard method for building co-occurrence networks — with the artifacts projection introduces (clique inflation); degree and degree sequences; subgraph families that matter — cliques, k-cores and core-periphery structure with k-core as a diffusion-relevant depth measure; triads, transitivity, and clustering coefficients (global and local, and their directed variants); motifs and the sociological reading of triad censuses; matrix methods — adjacency powers as walk counts and spectral properties previewed; core algorithms with cost awareness — traversal (BFS/DFS), shortest paths (Dijkstra, Bellman-Ford scope); and the practitioner calibration of reading results versus reading proofs.

**Absorbed Skill:** Compute and interpret paths, components, distances, and connectivity in directed and undirected graphs, project bipartite data knowing its clique-inflation artifacts, apply k-core decomposition and triad/motif analysis to structural questions, use adjacency matrix methods for walk counting, and select graph algorithms with computational cost awareness.

**Section 3 — Centrality: Position, Brokerage, and Influence**

**Summary:** Covers the most-used and most-abused toolkit: degree centrality with its directed split (in-degree as prestige, out-degree as expansiveness); betweenness centrality as brokerage measurement, closeness as independence-from-others, and their instability on sampled or incomplete graphs; eigenvector family — eigenvector centrality, Katz, PageRank with its damping parameter and search-engine history, and HITS hubs-and-authorities; the central lesson that centrality is a hypothesis family, not a single number — each measure answers a different question about position (reach, control, prestige) and they measurably disagree; weighted, temporal, and local variants at overview; the influence-identification literature — whether central nodes actually spread more (degree often suffices per Kempe-lineage results; the spreaders-versus-susceptibles distinction; retweet-influence studies showing retweetability is content-driven); practical influence ranking for trend work — who to monitor and who to seed, and why the answers differ; and the pitfalls — centrality computed on API-partial graphs, follower counts as manipulable signals, and verified-account confounds.

**Absorbed Skill:** Compute degree, betweenness, closeness, eigenvector, and PageRank centralities and state what question each answers, interpret disagreement among centrality measures on real networks, evaluate influence claims using spreadability-versus-position evidence, rank nodes for monitoring versus seeding with sampling awareness, and avoid centrality misuse on incomplete or manipulated platform graphs.

**Section 4 — Community Detection and Group Structure**

**Summary:** Teaches finding the tribes inside networks: the community concept and its definitional ambiguity — no single ground truth; modularity defined, optimized, and its resolution limit (small communities invisible at scale); algorithms in working depth — Girvan-Newman divisive betweenness, Louvain and Leiden aggregation (including Leiden's fix for badly-connected Louvain output), label propagation, and Infomap's flow/map-equation objective; overlapping communities and fuzzy membership at overview (link communities, bigCLAM lineage); hierarchical structure — dendrograms and multi-resolution partitions; validation without ground truth — internal metrics, perturbation stability, and qualitative reading of community content; interpretation discipline — algorithmic communities are not automatically scenes, echo chambers, or markets; structural equivalence and blockmodels at overview as role analysis distinct from community membership; community evolution in dynamic networks — growth, merge, split, death tracking as a trend-detection instrument; practical workflows in igraph/leidenalg/cdlib and Gephi; and common failure modes — resolution artifacts, singleton absorption, and over-interpreting unstable partitions.

**Absorbed Skill:** Apply Louvain/Leiden, Infomap, and label propagation with understanding of each objective, interpret modularity with resolution-limit awareness, validate communities through stability testing and qualitative reading, track community evolution over time as a trend signal, and distinguish algorithmic partitions from social phenomena like scenes and echo chambers.

**Section 5 — Network Models: Nulls, Small Worlds, and Statistical Families**

**Summary:** Covers models as the testing and explanation layer: why null models matter — structure is only surprising relative to a baseline; Erdős-Rényi random graphs — their properties (binomial degrees, sharp giant-component transition) and the catalog of ways real networks differ (heavy tails, clustering, assortativity); the configuration model as the practical degree-preserving null for significance testing; small worlds — Milgram's letter experiment read critically (completion bias), Watts-Strogatz rewiring producing high clustering with short paths, and small-worldness measurement practice; statistical network models — stochastic block models and degree-corrected variants as the modern community-inference workhorses, exponential random graph models (ERGMs) with their what-generates-structure question and degeneracy history; latent-space and network-regression approaches at overview; generative versus statistical versus mechanistic modeling distinguished; fitting, simulation, and goodness-of-fit practice; and using nulls for trend claims — testing whether an observed co-mention cluster exceeds degree-preserving expectation.

**Absorbed Skill:** Construct and apply ER, configuration-model, and Watts-Strogatz nulls to test structural claims, fit stochastic block models and interpret ERGM output with degeneracy awareness, measure clustering and small-worldness appropriately, select model families by explanatory question, and run simulation-based significance tests on observed network structure.

**Section 6 — Scale-Free Networks, Power Laws, and Attention Inequality**

**Summary:** Teaches the heavy-tail revolution with modern rigor: power-law degree distributions — definition, the log-log visual trap, and preferential attachment as the generating mechanism (Barabási-Albert model, Price's cumulative advantage lineage, fitness extensions); hubs and their systemic consequences — robustness-yet-fragility previewed, and hub dominance of diffusion reach; the measurement revolution — Clauset-Shalizi-Newman maximum-likelihood fitting with goodness-of-fit and comparison against log-normal and stretched-exponential alternatives, and the field-wide finding that many claimed power laws fail careful testing; the universality debate — Broido-Clauset's reanalysis showing scale-free structure is rare in real networks and the current nuanced consensus; rich-get-richer dynamics beyond degree — attention inequality on content platforms, viral concentration statistics (most content spreads minimally), and superstar economics (Rosen) as the market-level pattern; exponent meaning for diffusion — how tail shape changes outbreak-size distributions; long-tail claims (Anderson) checked against empirical distributions; and measurement practice — how sampling and API truncation distort tails.

**Absorbed Skill:** Test heavy-tail claims with MLE fitting, goodness-of-fit, and alternative-distribution comparison, explain preferential attachment and fitness mechanisms for hub formation, evaluate scale-free universality claims in light of the current debate, reason about how degree-distribution shape affects diffusion outcomes, and correct for sampling and truncation bias when measuring platform-graph tails.

**Section 7 — Diffusion and Contagion on Networks**

**Summary:** Covers the core mechanics of how ideas spread: epidemic-style compartment models on networks — SI, SIS, SIR — with network-dependent thresholds (the spectral-radius condition intuition) and why homogeneous-mixing models misestimate spread on clustered, heavy-tailed graphs; discrete cascade models — independent cascade and linear threshold (Kempe's formalization) and their assumptions; simple versus complex contagion (Centola-Macy) — information that spreads on single exposure versus behaviors requiring social reinforcement, with the experimental evidence and the clustering paradox (clusters help complex contagion, hinder simple); weak ties — Granovetter's bridges carrying novel information; threshold models of collective behavior (Granovetter 1978) and informational cascades (Bikhchandani-lineage) as the classic foundations; cascade data analysis — reconstructing diffusion trees from timestamps, cascade-size distributions and their heavy tails, and broadcast-shaped versus virally-shaped cascades; competing and cooperating memes — co-diffusion and interference at overview; and the contagion-metaphor audit — homophily-versus-influence confounding (the Christakis-Fowler obesity-contagion claims and the Shalizi-Thomas critique) as the standing warning for trend claims.

**Absorbed Skill:** Implement and analyze SIS/SIR and cascade/threshold diffusion models on networks, distinguish simple from complex contagion and predict which regime a behavior occupies, reconstruct diffusion trees from timestamped spread data and classify cascade shapes, diagnose homophily-versus-influence confounds in observed contagion claims, and apply weak-tie and threshold reasoning to information-versus-behavior spread questions.

**Section 8 — Influence Maximization, Seeding, and Viral Strategy**

**Summary:** Teaches the applied optimization problem and its reality checks: influence maximization formalized — the Kempe-Kleinberg-Tardos result (NP-hard, greedy algorithm with the 1−1/e submodularity guarantee) and its assumptions (known graph, known cascade model); scalable alternatives — degree heuristics, discounting for overlap, sketch-based methods (IMM) — and when simple beats optimal; the theory-practice gap — platform algorithmic curation sits between seed and audience, graphs are partially observable, and cascade models mis-specify real spread; the empirical virality record — most content fails to spread, early dynamics weakly predict cascade shape (Cheng-lineage Reddit studies), and the Watts influentials experiment showing context beats centrality for reach; seeding design matched to contagion type — bridge-node targeting for novel information versus community-embedded reinforcement for behavior adoption; measurement of campaigns — contamination controls, holdouts, and attribution honesty; ethical lines — legitimate amplification versus astroturfing and coordinated inauthentic behavior; and the strategy synthesis — what network position, content quality, timing, and platform mechanics each contribute to spread probability.

**Absorbed Skill:** Formulate influence maximization and apply greedy and heuristic solutions with submodularity understanding, design seeding strategies matched to simple-versus-complex contagion regimes, evaluate viral marketing claims against the empirical record including influentials findings, predict cascade potential from early dynamics features, and distinguish ethical amplification from manipulation in campaign design.

**Section 9 — Social Network Analysis: Sociological Method and Data**

**Summary:** Covers the research tradition behind the math: SNA lineage — Moreno's sociometry, the Hawthorne studies, and the Manchester school's situational analysis; network data collection — the boundary-specification problem (who counts as inside the network), roster versus name-generator instruments, position versus relational data, and digital-trace substitutes; ego-network analysis — alters, alter-alter ties, composition and structure measures — as the method when whole networks are unavailable; whole-network survey practice and its attrition problems; two-mode affiliation data (events, groups, hashtags) and projection practice; multiplexity and tie strength — Granovetter's strength construct and its measurement via frequency, intimacy, and duration; sampling hidden populations — snowball and respondent-driven sampling with estimator caveats; software lineage and modern practice — UCINET/NodeXL to Python/R stacks, and Gephi visualization craft including layout choices (ForceAtlas2) and how layouts mislead; documentation and reproducibility for network studies; and relational-data ethics — the third-party privacy problem (non-consenting alters in egos' data).

**Absorbed Skill:** Design network data collection with boundary specification and instrument choice, conduct ego-network and whole-network analyses appropriately, work with affiliation data, multiplexity, and tie-strength measures, visualize networks with layout literacy, and handle the sampling, missingness, and third-party-privacy issues specific to relational data.

**Section 10 — Temporal, Multilayer, and Higher-Order Networks**

**Summary:** Teaches structure beyond static snapshots: temporal networks — time-stamped contacts, temporal paths and reachability (why static analysis overestimates what can spread), time-respecting ordering, and the aggregation trade-off; burstiness of human communication — inter-event-time distributions and the burstiness parameter, with consequences for diffusion timing; temporal centrality and community evolution methods; multilayer and multiplex networks — the same actors across platforms, interlayer coupling, and cross-platform meme migration (the Reddit-to-TikTok-to-Instagram pipeline as the working example) analyzed in multilayer terms; higher-order interactions — hypergraphs and simplicial complexes capturing group interactions irreducible to pairs, and higher-order contagion results (simplicial thresholds changing outbreak dynamics); dynamic graph representations — event lists versus snapshot sequences; data realities — observation windows, API history limits, and censoring; tooling — temporal network libraries and multilayer frameworks at working level; and the judgment call — when temporal and multilayer complexity changes conclusions versus when aggregated static analysis suffices.

**Absorbed Skill:** Analyze temporal networks with time-respecting paths and aggregation trade-offs, quantify interaction burstiness and its diffusion consequences, model cross-platform spread as multilayer networks, apply higher-order interaction thinking where group dynamics matter, and judge when temporal and multilayer complexity is analytically necessary.

**Section 11 — Platform Graphs in Practice: Follows, Cascades, and Co-Engagement**

**Summary:** Covers the trend analyst's actual network data: graph types distinguished — follow/friendship graphs (partially observable, manipulable), interaction graphs (replies, mentions, quote-posts), diffusion graphs (repost and share cascades with timestamps), and co-engagement/affiliation graphs (co-following, co-hashtag, co-viewing) — with each type's analytic affordances and validity limits; cascade analysis practice — hashtag and challenge propagation (TikTok sounds and dances as participation cascades), meme-template reuse networks, and the structural signatures of broadcast versus viral versus community-driven spread; recommendation-mediation — the modern fact that most apparent social spread is algorithmically routed through feeds, and methods for separating network effects from platform amplification; influencer identification with fake-follower and engagement-pod detection; discourse-community mapping and bridge detection between topical groups; data-access realities — API tiers, deprecations, firehose economics, and vendor graphs; graph storage and querying — graph databases (Neo4j/Cypher at working level) and big-graph engineering; and assembling a platform-network monitoring stack for trend intelligence.

**Absorbed Skill:** Distinguish follow, interaction, diffusion, and co-engagement graphs with their analytic limits, analyze repost and participation cascades including shape classification, separate network spread from algorithmic amplification in observed diffusion, build influencer and community monitoring with fake-engagement detection, and engineer platform-graph collection and storage under real API constraints.

**Section 12 — Robustness, Percolation, and System Fragility**

**Summary:** Teaches resilience through the network lens: node and edge removal experiments — random failure versus targeted attack on hubs, the Albert-Jeong-Barabási robustness-yet-fragility finding, and removal curves as robustness measures; percolation on networks — giant-component emergence and disappearance, critical thresholds, and the mapping between percolation and epidemic thresholds; cascading failure models — load redistribution and threshold cascades with infrastructure analogies (power grids) and platform analogies (API dependency chains); sociotechnical fragility — bridge-node vulnerability (who holds discourse communities together), key-person risk in movements and brands, and platform-dependency risk for monitoring operations; superspreaders and overdispersion — the k-parameter view of why most seeds spark nothing and few ignite everything; robustness interventions — redundancy, diverse seeding, multi-platform presence — and their costs; the robustness-versus-resilience distinction (resistance to shock versus recovery after it); and applying fragility analysis to trend systems themselves — what breaks a monitoring pipeline when an API or platform changes.

**Absorbed Skill:** Run and interpret removal and percolation experiments on real networks, distinguish robustness from resilience in networked systems, identify structural vulnerabilities (bridges, hubs, superspreader potential) in brand, movement, and platform graphs, design redundancy and diverse-seeding strategies with cost awareness, and assess fragility of the trend-monitoring systems themselves.

**Section 13 — Network Epistemology: Echo Chambers, Polarization, and Misinformation Spread**

**Summary:** Covers belief and information as network phenomena: precise definitions — echo chambers (social homophily plus active exclusion of contrary sources) versus filter bubbles (algorithmic personalization) — and the evidence status of each (exposure studies showing most online exposure is more diverse than assumed, with cross-cutting-exposure findings and recent reassessments); measuring segregation and fragmentation in platform data; polarization — affective versus issue-based, and network signatures of each; opinion-dynamics models — DeGroot averaging, bounded-confidence models (Deffuant, Hegselmann-Krause), and stubborn-agent variants as tools for reasoning about belief convergence and divergence; misinformation diffusion findings — the Vosoughi-Roy-Aral falsehood-spreads-farther result with its novelty-and-emotion mechanisms and the interpretation debates it spawned; radicalization-pathway claims (YouTube rabbit holes) and their mixed empirical record; epistemic network analysis at overview; interventions — bridging exposure experiments and documented backfire effects; and the trend-analyst application — tracking narrative communities and their divergence as an ecosystem-level indicator.

**Absorbed Skill:** Define and measure echo chambers, filter bubbles, and polarization with evidence-status precision, apply opinion-dynamics models to belief-spread questions, analyze misinformation diffusion findings including interpretation controversies, evaluate exposure and bridging interventions with backfire awareness, and track discourse fragmentation as an ecosystem indicator.

**Section 14 — Tooling, Scale, and Reproducible Network Practice**

**Summary:** Builds professional craft: the Python/R network stack in depth — NetworkX for prototyping and algorithm richness with memory limits, igraph for performance, graph-tool for heavy computation, cdlib for community methods — and selection criteria; large-graph handling — sparse matrices, graph databases and query languages, streaming updates, and graph sampling methods (snowball, forest-fire) with estimator bias; visualization practice — Gephi workflows, layout algorithms and their distortions, network figures that communicate findings versus decorate, and interactive tooling (pyvis, Cytoscape.js); performance patterns — vectorization, edge-list discipline, parallelized community detection; reproducibility — seeds, versioned graph artifacts, documented pipelines, and small-known-graph unit tests for network code; integration with the wider trend stack — NLP entities feeding co-mention graphs, cascade data feeding diffusion models, network features feeding forecasts; collaboration artifacts — sharing graphs, notebooks, and figure standards; and the cost discipline of not building graph infrastructure when a dataframe suffices.

**Absorbed Skill:** Select network libraries and databases matched to scale and task, sample and estimate on large graphs with bias awareness, produce communicative network visualizations with layout literacy, build reproducible and tested network pipelines, and integrate graph analysis with NLP and cascade components of a trend stack.

**Section 15 — Synthesis and Frontier: Graph ML, Causal Networks, and Trend Intelligence**

**Summary:** Prepares expert integration: graph machine learning — node embeddings (DeepWalk, node2vec) and graph neural networks (message-passing intuition, GCN/GraphSAGE) for link prediction and node classification, with honest assessment of when GNNs beat simple structural features; link prediction as early-signal detection (who will connect next — emerging ties between communities as a trend precursor); causal inference on networks — interference and SUTVA violations, exposure mappings, cluster-randomized and network-randomized designs, and the homophily-influence identification problem with experimental solutions (Aral-lineage work); simulation as laboratory — calibrating agent-based diffusion models against observed cascades and using them for intervention counterfactuals; combining diffusion models with time-series forecasting of spread trajectories; LLM-and-network frontiers — LLM reasoning over graphs and its current limits, networks of interacting agents; federated and open-protocol networks (ActivityPub, AT Protocol) changing graph access and structure; privacy-preserving network analysis (differentially private graph release) at awareness; and the capstone — a complete network trend-intelligence project: acquire interaction data, build and validate graphs, run community, diffusion, and vulnerability analyses, identify emerging clusters and spread dynamics, and deliver a monitored, reproducible report with causal humility.

**Absorbed Skill:** Apply node embeddings and GNNs where they beat structural baselines, use link prediction as an early-signal method for emerging connections, design network-aware causal studies handling interference and homophily-influence identification, calibrate diffusion simulations against observed cascades, and deliver complete network trend-intelligence projects with monitoring and causal humility.

## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Covers the classic predictors with their evidence status: the five attributes — relative advantage (perceived betterment over the superseded idea, including economic, social-prestige, convenience, and satisfaction dimensions), compatibility (fit with existing values, past experiences, and needs of potential adopters), complexity (difficulty of understanding and use, the most consistently negative predictor), trialability (experimentability on a limited basis), and observability (visibility of results to others); the attribute framework's evidence — Rogers' synthesis that these five explain a substantial share of adoption-rate variance, tempered by the documented critical reassessments (Downes-Mui finding attribute effects weaker and less consistent than the canon suggests, and the meta-analytic record varying by attribute and context); attribute measurement — survey scaling practice (semantic differential instruments), and the perception-not-objectivity principle (engineered simplicity matters less than perceived simplicity); attribute management — how marketers and product teams change adoption rates by changing perceptions: increasing relative advantage framing, compatibility bridging (adapter products, migration paths), complexity reduction (onboarding design), trialability engineering (freemium, samples, test drives), and observability amplification (visible use, social sharing features, public results); the superseded-idea analysis — every innovation diffuses against an incumbent (behavior, product, or none), and the comparison target determines the attribute perceptions; attribute interactions and trade-offs — highly advantageous innovations often carry complexity costs, and the net-configuration analysis; category-specific attribute profiles — which attributes dominate for consumer gadgets versus health behaviors versus enterprise software; the cultural layer — compatibility's dependence on local values making attribute assessments region-specific; and the attribute diagnostic — scoring an innovation's five attributes against its target segment and incumbent to explain and forecast its adoption rate.

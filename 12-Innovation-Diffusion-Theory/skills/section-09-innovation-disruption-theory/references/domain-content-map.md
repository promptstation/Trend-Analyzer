## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Covers the most influential and most misapplied theory in innovation management: the core model — sustaining innovations (improving established products for existing customers along known performance dimensions) versus disruptive innovations (initially inferior on mainstream metrics but superior on new dimensions — simplicity, convenience, price, accessibility — entering through low-end or new-market footholds that incumbents rationally ignore, then improving upmarket until the mainstream follows); the two disruption types — low-end (overserved customers defect to good-enough cheaper options) and new-market (non-consumers converted by accessible solutions), with the classic cases (steel minimills, discount retail, personal computers, and the documented case canon); the incumbent's dilemma — why rational management (listening to best customers, pursuing high margins) systematically misses disruptive threats, and the organizational response (autonomous units, separate value networks); the theory's precision requirements — Christensen's explicit criteria (foothold segment, new value dimensions, upmarket trajectory) that exclude most products loosely called disruptive (ride-hailing and many platform launches fail the tests per published analyses); the critique record — Lepore's historical challenge to the case evidence, King-Baatartogtokh's HBR examination finding most 'disruptive' label uses don't fit the model, and the survivorship-bias concern (countless failed footholds never theorized); what survives — the value-network insight, the asymmetric-motivation logic, and foothold-then-improve as a documented pattern class, held with scope conditions; applying the tests — the disruption-diagnosis checklist for any claimed disruptor; the trend-analyst use — identifying genuinely disruptive entries early by their signature (worse on mainstream metrics, better on new ones, growing in overlooked segments) and forecasting their upmarket trajectory; disruption adjacent concepts — architectural versus modular innovation, competence-destroying versus competence-enhancing change; and the discipline — reserving the term for model-conforming cases and using diffusion and S-curve language elsewhere.

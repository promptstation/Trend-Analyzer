## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Teaches movements as organized driver forces: social movements defined — sustained collective campaigns making claims on authorities, distinguished from fads, riots, and interest groups; the theoretical toolkit — resource mobilization (movements need organization, money, skills, not just grievance), political process theory (opportunities: elite division, access points, allies in power — movements rise when politics opens), and framing theory (Benford-Snow: diagnostic, prognostic, motivational frames that make grievances actionable and resonant); movement lifecycles — emergence, coalescence, bureaucratization, and decline/abeyance, with the outcomes spectrum (success, co-optation, failure, spin-off); tactics and their evolution — from marches and strikes to boycotts, culture jamming, hashtag campaigns, and platform-native actions, each with attention and pressure logics (connecting to the attention course); movement repression and backlash — state and counter-movement responses, and the radicalization-flank effects; digital mobilization — platforms lowering organizing costs, speed-versus-depth trade-offs ('tactical freeze' and leaderless fragility findings), and slacktivism debates resolved toward hybrid online-offline organization; movement success conditions — the documented correlates (large sustained participation, elite allies, favorable public opinion, disruptive capacity) that let analysts assess which movements will move markets; countermovements — every movement breeds opposition, and the dialectic drives culture-war trend dynamics; movement-to-institution pathways — when movements become parties, NGOs, regulations, or market norms (sustainability movement to ESG regime as the completed arc); and movement mapping — the analyst's assessment of any movement's resources, opportunities, frames, tactics, lifecycle stage, and market-relevant targets.
